Module 10

Agentic AI Governance

When AI systems can act, mistakes become actions. Governance is the set of policies, technical controls, and human responsibilities that keep agents safe, reliable, and accountable.

About 40 minutes3 Python examplesAll levels

Why agents need governance

A chatbot's wrong answer can be ignored. An agent's wrong action might send an email, change a record, or spend money. Agents also face new risks: instructions hidden in web pages or documents (prompt injection), tools used in unintended ways, and long chains of steps where small errors compound. Governance answers practical questions: What may this agent do? Who approves risky actions? How do we know it works? What happens when it fails?

Nine areas of agent governance

Safety and reliability

Agents behave as intended, fail safely, and stay within limits.

In practice: guardrails, timeouts, step and budget limits, safe defaults.

Transparency and traceability

People can see what the agent did and why.

In practice: step-by-step traces, cited sources, disclosure that users talk to AI.

Privacy and security

Personal and sensitive data are protected; attacks are anticipated.

In practice: data minimization, PII redaction, secrets outside prompts, injection defenses.

Human oversight

People can approve, correct, or stop the agent.

In practice: approval for risky actions, review queues, a working stop button.

Access control

The agent has only the permissions its task needs.

In practice: tool allowlists, read-only credentials, per-user permissions, spending caps.

Evaluation and validation

Quality and safety are measured before and after release.

In practice: test sets, red teaming, release gates, regression tests after changes.

Accountability

Named people own the system and its outcomes.

In practice: owners per agent, documented decisions, audit logs, clear escalation paths.

Monitoring and incident response

Problems are detected quickly and handled in a planned way.

In practice: dashboards, alerts, kill switches, incident playbooks, post-incident reviews.

Responsible deployment

Rollout matches the evidence and the risk.

In practice: pilots, staged rollouts, user training, impact assessment, feedback channels.

Limits of pattern matching

Keyword rules are easy to bypass, for example by rephrasing or using another language. Treat them as one layer among several, alongside permission limits, model-based classifiers, and human review.

Incident response for agents

  1. Detect: an alert fires, or a user reports a problem.
  2. Contain: pause the agent or disable the affected tool (a kill switch should take seconds).
  3. Investigate: use traces to find what input, step, and tool caused the issue.
  4. Fix: change prompts, permissions, guardrails, or code, and add the case to your test set.
  5. Re-evaluate and restore: run the full evaluation before turning the agent back on.
  6. Learn: write a short blameless review and inform affected users if needed.

Governance frameworks and guidance

  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework

    Voluntary U.S. framework organized around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage.

  • EU Artificial Intelligence Act (explorer site)

    A risk-based European regulation for AI systems, with obligations by risk level.

  • ISO/IEC 42001

    International standard for an AI management system in an organization.

  • OECD AI Principles

    Intergovernmental principles for trustworthy AI.

  • OWASP GenAI Security Project

    Home of the OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications, including prompt injection and excessive agency.

  • Saudi Data and AI Authority (SDAIA)

    National authority for data and AI in Saudi Arabia, which publishes AI ethics principles and guidance.
